T
HE players of an RPG have a shared goal, to create a living imaginative world, inhabited
by characters that they, in turn, inhabit as role-players. It is the responsibility of all the
players to add richness and depth to the world, so that the characters we inhabit can
intuitively interact with it, such that not only will there be nothing to break the mental picture
of the fiction we hold in our imagination, but that the fictional world, and inhabiting the char-
acters within it, will be rewarding in a way that no other medium provides. This responsibil-
ity is shared by all the players, including the GM.
THE PLAYERS
You are there to role-play a player character
(PC). To do that, you narrate what your
character does in real-time, and you speak
the words that they speak. Within the play-
ers’ domain is a) your character (represented
mechanically by a character sheet, reified by
you), b) your character’s past actions, and c)
the pieces of the world they interact with.
What’s outside of your domain is the other
characters, their past actions, and the pieces
of the world they are interacting with.
It’s your job as a player to keep things mov-
ing. If the game slows down, it is your
fault. People with interesting lives do not
just sit around waiting for someone else to
guide them to their goals, they act, they
move, they instigate.
It’s your job to know your characters and
know the rules of the game. Why? Well, be-
cause these things facilitate role-play.
THE GM
Which stands for Gesellschaft Mensch, is just
one of the players. Like the other players
they have one primary responsibility, and
that, is to role-play. The difference is, that
the other players get one character each, and
the GM gets to role-play everybody that isn’t
the player characters, that is, the non-player
characters (NPCs).
If the GM so much as tells the players what
their player character feels or sees, they are
overstepping their bounds. You can tell them
what’s there, just not their experience of it.
DO NOT MOVE THE PLAYER CHARAC-
TERS, DO NOT TELL THEM WHAT THEY
DO. DO NOT TELEPORT THEM ANY-
WHERE, DO NOT REITERATE, RE-CON-
TEXTUALISE OR RE-DESCRIBE THEIR AC-
TIONS. THE PLAYER CHARACTERS ARE
OUTSIDE YOUR DOMAIN. LET THE PLAY-
ERS PLAY THE GAME.
If the players decide to do nothing of note,
they will have made a dull bed for them-
selves. You just take your characters and have
them move right on ahead with their own
plans.
Since the GM’s domain includes the past ac-
tions of their own characters (which is ev-
eryone except the player characters), they get
the lion’s share of deciding what has hap-
pened in the fictional world before you start
playing, but they are not in any sense the
‘master’ of the game (dungeon or otherwise),
or any kind of storyteller or director or au-
thor.
Because of the breadth of this domain, you
will often be describing the world, so try to
keep descriptions short and to the point,
making sure to feature The Obvious and The
Unusual. Based on your description, the play-
ers will be free to interact with the world as
they imagine it, so make sure your descrip-
tions are clear and concise. It’ll take practice,
don’t worry, just roll with it if the players in-
teract with it in an incongruous way.
You also get to set the Difficulty of rolls that
the players make, based on the circumstances
and what the players are choosing to do.
