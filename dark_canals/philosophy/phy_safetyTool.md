Rpgs allow us to explore the imaginary, to explore whatever our minds can come up
with. Unfortunately our minds are generally cesspools, clogged with all manner of
trauma inducing psychological detritus from a lifetime of maladaptive behaviours,
which is a shame, because RPGs seem like the perfect place to explore and learn and grow.
Maybe we can salvage something of this potential by understanding the people we are (and
play with).
THE GYMNASIUM
In order to build muscle, you need to use
those muscles, and not only that, but you
have to push beyond your current capacity,
lifting heavier and heavier weights in order
to break down your current muscles and let
them regrow stronger.
Our response to trauma is like a muscle, it
has to be challenged in order to grow, and
the more we do this, the more confident we
become when facing things that once caused
us trepidation.
Just like with our muscles though, different
people have different thresholds for trauma.
One ‘weight’ might not be enough to chal-
lenge one person, but might be perfect to get
someone else to grow, and one weight that’s
enough to help someone grow might be
enough to crush someone completely, caus-
ing an irreparable injury from which they
never recover.
FINDING THE THRESHOLD THAT WILL
HELP SOMEONE TO GROW AND RE-
COVER FROM TRAUMA IS THE JOB OF A
TRAINED MEDICAL PROFESSIONAL. NOT
THE GM OF A ROLE-PLAYING GAME.
RPGS ARE NOT THERAPY.
That being said, an RPG can still be a great,
safe, rewarding way of exploring our psyche,
it can still be therapeutic, so long as we’re
comfortable that we’re in no danger of cross-
ing that threshold. If a group of players have
significantly different thresholds, then in or-
der to satisfy one player you might have to
exclude the very thing another player needs
in order to get anything out of playing at all.
Put bluntly, these players may simply be in-
compatible.
PRIMUM NON NOCERE
The intent of tools to allow people to inter-
ject during a game and tailor the content to
their personal thresholds appears to come
from the noble principle of favouring non-
maleficence (not doing harm) over benefi-
cence (doing good). Fair enough. Ideally
though, you should just get this stuff out of
the way beforehand, in the same way you
would with a piece of media you can’t inter-
ject into, like a film.
You wouldn’t willingly go see a R rated hor-
ror film if you didn’t think you could handle
it, likewise, you probably don’t want to play
in an RPG with similars premises and con-
cepts. So don’t! Check the classification of
your game (or in other words, write or re-
view your table’s social contract) before en-
gaging with it, and only go into it if you’re
willing to engage with that level of content.
Dark Canals explicitly involves bloody vio-
lence, monsters and death. If you wouldn’t
go see a film like that, don’t play a game like
that. Maybe another game like Carp Banal,
the river fishing sim would be better. Or
Harp Corral, the cowboy musician sim. Or
Mart Cabal, the wizard convenience store
sim. Or Tart Halal the middle-eastern bakery
sim. Or Dart Royale, the monarchist pub
sports tournament sim. Or something else.
