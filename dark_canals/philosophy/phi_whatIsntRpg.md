If you've never played a role-playing game (or 'RPG') before, thank God - new players tend
to be better at role-playing than those of us that have years of bad habits to unlearn.
In short, a role-playing game is where you inhabit a character in a shared imaginary world
with other role-players, you make believe that you are that character, doing and saying what
you think they'd say or do. We do this because it's rewarding to explore being someone else,
exploring another world; we do it because it's rewarding to imagine a fictional world where
we have agency. We do it to find out what might happen in interesting 'what if?' scenarios.
There are a few things that often get confused with the role-playing game, as that term gets
bandied about quite a lot, though, so let's clear those up...

## STORYTELLING?
Many other RPGs also tout that they are Col-
laborative Storytelling Games, or something to
that effect, sometimes even claiming that all
RPGs simply are collaborative storytelling
games. Despite being one potential use for
them though, imposition of plot, narrative
structure, or authorial or directorial control
are not necessary for a satisfying role-play
experience. In fact, for us, those things posi-
tively ruin the game world’s sense of reality
and have us rolling our eyes every time they
crop up.
The goal here is not to construct a narrative.
Think about how you act in real life. You
make decisions, you take action, you have
goals and motivations and you are able to tell
stories about your life, but you're able to do
this without anyone attempting to mould
your life into a narratively satisfying arc, or
impose a three act structure by manipulating
the consequences of your actions. This is be-
cause, in life, we form stories by looking back
on events and selecting a series of them that
have meaning to us. This important process
is part of how we make sense of our lives,
how we find meaning in its interconnected-
ness - and, we think, that this is your right as
the agent of a character, that you play the
role of, in an RPG.
It is not up to some authorial storyteller to
tell you what would make the best story for
your character (as if they'd know), by decid-
ing in advance the structure it will take or
plot some points for it to follow. Likewise,
just like in life, for you to play a role you act
with agency, not with executive narrative
control. If you want to construct a narrative,
do so - there are countless suitable mediums
for this (a collaborative storytelling game,
perhaps?), but if you want to play the role of
a character, in the same sense that you do in
life, then that is what this kind of game is for!

## ACTING?
An actor playing a fierce barbarian might
need to learn how to swing a sword in order
to give a convincing performance - how they
move, how they speak, the delivery is a cru-
cial part of acting. In a novel though, there is
no delivery - the very same character comes
alive in your imagination based on a descrip-
tion of what they do and how they do it. To
be a good role-player, you do not need to be
an expert at delivery, as you are not giving a
performance. What matters is what you say
and do, not how you might embody it, be-
cause an RPG plays out in the imagination.
You don't need to put on a funny voice, or
dress like your character, or be like them
physically at all! What you need to do to play
the role well is to make sure the character
acts in the way you think they should based
on their concept, their goals, and their moti-
vations and to react to the imaginary world
as events unfold. What your character does
(and says), and how they do it (or say it), is
not based on your ability to do those things,
so don't get caught up on giving a perfor-
mance, just stay in your character, and try to
see the world as they would.

## A COMPETITION?
Many board games and war games (which
share gameplay elements and history with
RPGs) are a competition between the play-
ers, with rules designed to mediate the con-
flict. In an RPG though, all the players share
the same goal - to create a living imaginary
world, and to see what happens within it.
The rules that make an RPG a 'game,' are not
there to make sure the competition between
the players is fair, they are there to facilitate
player agency - to allow you to act within the
game world in a way that makes sense. If we
can't understand the world our character in-
habits, or if the world doesn't respond to our
decisions in a comprehensible way, we can't
make meaningful decisions, and thus cannot
act with agency, and thus cannot role-play!
Unlike the make-believe of our school-yard
days, or the freewheeling nature of improvi-
sational theatre, the table-top RPG needs
mechanics to make sure the world runs on a
set of semi-predictable rules (kind of like the
real world) because the world will feel more
real if it does, and allow us to act within it in
a very human and intuitive way.

## A FILM?
Cinema is a such a powerful medium that ev-
eryone from novelists, video game develop-
ers, photographers and sometimes even mu-
sicians seek to give their audience a Cinematic
experience.

> "If we only see comics in relation to movies then the bes that they will ever be is films that do not move"
> Alan Moore

Often, by adding a social component to
something, we can rely on the natural plea-
sures of human interaction to create the en-
3
joyment we derive from the pastime. A
multi-player video-game might be objec-
tively worse than its single-player counter-
part, but it doesn't matter - it's fun because
we're playing it with our friends.
Far too often RPGs are treated like an objec-
tively worse way to experience something
like watching a film, but we tolerate it be-
cause it can be more social than watching a
film. Like Moore's view on comics though,
we believe that RPGs are not just the poor
man's cinema but the objectively best way to
engage in an experience distinct from those
other mediums, that of role-play.
In an RPG we want to give you the experi-
ence of being a character in a fictional world,
not being a character in a film that is set in a
fictional world.

## AN OLD-SCHOOL VIDEO GAME?
As the vinyl record made way for the conve-
nience of the Compact Disc (and now stream-
ing), people started to see the former
medium as quaint and archaic. It was fun to
revisit the past, but the world has moved on,
and for good reason.
With the advent of role-playing games in the
video game sphere, streamlining inventory
management and doing all the math for us, it
seemed to some that the pen-and-paper RPG
was just a quaint and archaic way of playing
a video game à la spreadsheet and abacus.
While video game RPGs have lots to offer
that come with the strengths of that
medium, table-top RPGs are not just an ana-
log way of playing them, and offer far more
in terms of freedom and agency. Put bluntly,
if your table-top RPG is just aping a video
game (if you're just playing Diablo but every
time you attack an enemy you have to solve
a math problem like some sort of edutainment
game), you aren't harnessing the unique
powers of the RPG medium, you aren't role-
playing, in the same sense you do in this kind
of game, you're just playing a worse version
of a video game!
