T
HE players inhabit a character in a shared imaginary world. They take turns saying
where they are and what they do. They speak in first person, and stay in character for
the entire session, only breaking momentarily to roll some dice and use the results to
inform how they continue to role-play. That’s how you play Dark Canals. Once the session is
over you can crack jokes and decompress and have a good yarn about what happened, what
you could do differently and how you might improve as a role-player. Not during, though.
That’s not the game.
STAY IN CHARACTER
You’d be surprised how many of the most
common gripes people have with RPGs are
solved by this. If there are no out-of-charac-
ter discussions, everything will go more
smoothly, more swiftly, it’ll be more intu-
itive and you’ll get so much more done. The
ideal you want to strive for is to get as much
done in an RPG session as would happen in
a film of the same length. Staying in charac-
ter is the way to get this sort of expediency!
BUT HOW DO I ASK THE GM QUESTIONS?
You don’t! Think about how you find stuff
out in real life. Do you ask a disembodied
voice in the ether? No! You act! You do stuff!
You look for an answer, or you ask someone!
Go forth and interact with the world.
BUT HOW DO I KNOW WHERE I AM?
It’s your character, you decide where they are
- where would they be, if this was all real? If
on every turn you say where you are, and if
you pay attention when the other players
speak, relative location becomes a non-issue.
BUT HOW DO I KNOW WHAT’S IN THE ROOM?
Either you look around, or, if by the descrip-
tions of the other players you imagine the
place having certain things in it, you can in-
teract with that stuff as intuitively as you do
with stuff in the room you’re in now as you
read this.
WHERE & WHAT
Every player should get a turn - it doesn’t re-
ally matter what order, whatever makes
sense, and on every turn, you should be say-
ing where you are and what you do. Every.
Single. Turn. And don’t take any longer say-
ing it that it would take to actually do. Your
turn should last up to about 30 seconds, so in
a group of 4-5 players, you should be saying
where you are and what you do every 2-3
minutes. Has it been 4 minutes and someone
else is still talking? Then do something! In
character, of course. Describe anything
you do in such a way as helps the other
players imagine it.
WHAT IF I DON’T GO ANYWHERE?
‘Where you are,’ isn’t just about movement,
it’s about your connection to the world, and
every turn is an opportunity to enrich that
world and your character. Even if you don’t
go anywhere, anchor yourself to the fiction by
describing some kind of interaction with it.
Your character isn’t a floating head, people
are always doing stuff, even if it’s subtle. Pat
someone on the shoulder when you talk to
them. Pick something up and play with it.
Eat a biscuit.
TAKING TIME
Real-time is the ideal, so the best way to tell
how long something takes is to picture it
happening, but there are a few things we can
do to better manage time in an RPG.
‘I’M GOING TO...’
Oh, yeah? When? Don’t tell us you’re going to
do something, just say ‘I [do the thing].’ This
habit of mincing words and being non-com-
mittal is generally a result of trying not to
trespass on an authoritarian GM’s vision of
what’s ‘supposed’ to happen. We don’t need
that here. If your GM read this book, they’re
better than that.

BE UNAFRAID
Be decisive. Be ready. You’ll never know ev-
ery factor affecting a decision you make, in
life or in the game, so just act. You can, of
course, deliberate with other characters, but
it should be in-character, it should be the
characters planning something, and if there’s
no time to do so because you’re being at-
tacked already? Well, you’ll have to work
that stuff out on the fly.
WHAT ARE YOU AFRAID OF? THAT THE
GM WILL SPRING SOME ‘GOTCHA’ MO-
MENT ON YOU BECAUSE YOU DIDN’T
ASK THE RIGHT SET OF COMPREHENSIVE
QUESTIONS? ARE YOU AFRAID SOME-
THING TERRIBLE WILL HAPPEN TO
YOUR CHARACTER? OH, THE HUMAN-
ITY!
Your GM’s goal is the same as yours, creating
a verisimilitudinous world, not to ‘win’
against the players on rules minutia techni-
calities. You’re all working together to make
a world that makes sense, so act with inten-
tion and you will be rewarded with the con-
sequences of your actions.
ROLL IT
So you’ve described what you’re doing, the
GM has indicated how difficult a task is, all
you have to do is roll some dice. The resolu-
tion mechanic is simple enough, so just pick
up those dice and roll them. In a way, seeing
success on the dice is just permission for you
to continue describing what your character
does, but for failure, or if success involves
the reaction of an NPC, as long as it’s obvious
to the GM what you rolled, you can move
straight on. A dice roll should be over in a
matter of seconds, don’t let it interrupt the
pace.

FAST-FORWARD
The GM is not going to tuck you in to bed. If
the day is done, you just say ‘I roll over and
fall asleep.’ Once everyone has done that, the
sun will soon be rising. If you’re about to
head on a long hike across barren terrain,
you can just say ‘I head north towards the
mountains.’ Once all the players have ex-
pressed a similar intention, uneventful time
can be seamlessly skipped, but remember, it’s
up to you as the player to telegraph the in-
tent of your character, not for the GM to
shuffle everyone around. Know when to end
the moment, and move on. Just don’t cause a
time-violation by narrating stuff that would
take far longer than real time to accomplish.
PATTY-CAKE
People rarely have time to suffer time-
wasters, and so it is with your characters. It’s
fine to talk to other characters about all sorts
of stuff, but it is easy to get caught in a loop
of talking about nothing, when there’s prob-
ably something more important you should
be doing. So check yourself regularly and if
things are getting stuck, take action.
A GOOD SESSION
If you’ve made an interesting character, that
lives in an interesting world, then you will
have an engaging session. You will get lost in
the fiction in the same way you do when
reading a good book, except that everything
that happened will be a result of your deci-
sions as your character. If you’re anything
like those of us that do it this way, you’ll be
aching to return to that world. This is only
the beginning though, there are so many
worlds to explore, so many characters, and a
long road of improvement as a role-player
ahead of us, and I look forward to seeing
where it leads. Good luck. Amor Fati.
