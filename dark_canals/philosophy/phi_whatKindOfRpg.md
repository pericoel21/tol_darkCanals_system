The term role-playing game is generally used to mean any game that features role-play-
ing (inhabiting a character, making decisions for them etc.) in any capacity. Some
games use role-playing as a tool with the goal of telling a story, some use it to add a lit-
tle context to hours and hours of tactical combat board-gaming. Dark Canals is interested in
neither of these things, as we are not here to pay lip service to the term RPG, but to exemplify
it; we do not want to earn that term by including role-play in just any capacity, but to as full
an extent as is practicable. In the same way that the genre of Heavy Metal has a sub-genre called
Heavy Metal, Dark Canals is not only a role-playing game, but also a Role-Playing Game.

## ANTHROPO-SIMULATIONIST
Dark Canals isn't trying to emulate the expe-
rience of being a character in a piece of narra-
tive fiction. It is instead trying to simulate the
fictional world that this kind of story might
take place in, but not in the way that most
people call simulationist. Rather than try to
simulate how the world works on some fun-
damental level, Dark Canals is instead con-
cerned with the way humans might experi-
ence that world, so the mechanics aim at sim-
ulating, or differentiating between, things
that are significant on a human perceptual
level.
If you need specialised equipment, or to do a
bunch of complicated math, in order to un-
derstand how something works in the world,
we probably aren't going to simulate it at that
level.

## REIFICATIONIST
Reification is the process of treating some-
thing abstract, like a thought or an idea, as
real or concrete. In the context of a TTRPG,
we use this term to mean making the me-
chanics ‘real’, treating them kind of like the
code that a video game runs on, then translat-
ing that code into the human experiential un-
derstanding of what the code represents (in
the same way that a graphics card and sound
card translate ones and zeroes into sights and
sounds).
Dark Canals is designed with this process in
mind, so that everything you might do in the
game can be translated into the experience of
playing the game, rather than having to just
regurgitate the game’s code, but it is your re-
sponsibility as a player of the game to com-
plete this process! Table-top role-playing
games don't have graphics cards, they have
you!
In the same way that we don't want to see a
video game's code while we're experiencing
the game, or to see studio lights and boom
microphones in shot while watching a film,
we don't want to see an RPG's code while
we're playing it either. While you're learning
the system, it might be necessary in order to
get it right, but you can be considered to have
System Mastery over the game once you no
longer need to make reference to it's code,
when through natural language you know
what mechanics are being invoked. If you're
in the midst of a game and forget some rule
or other, just go with your gut and use your
intuition to keep the game moving and look
it up afterwards, just don't let the game slow
down, or worse, crash because the code failed
to run properly!

There is a certain pleasure to be derived from
peeking behind the screen, as it were, to see
how games run, or how a film is made, but
that's not the primary way we want to actu-
ally experience the thing. When you recom-
mend someone watch a film that you like,
you don't generally recommend putting on
the director commentary for a first viewing,
or the picture-in-picture behind the scenes
gallery experience special feature overlay. You
want them to experience it as it was intended
to be experienced. Well, a session of Dark
Canals is intended to be experienced com-
pletely reified.
VERISIMILITUDINOUS
Verisimilitude is the 'truthiness' of a work of
fiction, how real it feels. Sometimes things
can be very realistic, that is, they go into great
detail to recreate how something is in reality,
but they don't feel real because that level of
detail is more than humans can intuitively
process. The more realistic you make some-
thing in one way, the less real it feels in an-
other. There's a couple of ways that can make
an RPG feel more real that often get ne-
glected, like the passage of time, and how we
visualise and interact with the imaginary
world.
THE PASSAGE OF TIME
Did you know that time passes in real-time?
Time can seem to slow down and speed up
depending on the circumstance, but this has
its limits. The slowest a role-playing game
should be, is as close to real-time as possible;
things should happen as close to the time it
takes to narrate them, or to visualise them, as
possible. For every increment slower than
real time the game is, the less verisimilitudi-
nous it will be.
Naturally, we don't want things to be as slow
in game as they are in real life when nothing
much is happening, so we can give as much
time to those things as, for example, we do
when we recount those moments from mem-
ory. For exciting moments though, of action
or tension, we don't want that ruined by tak-
ing an hour to play out a board game version
of what's happening, and the absolute best
way to keep things moving is to pay atten-

tion, and stay in character and just say what
you're doing when it's your turn to do so, and
shut up and listen when it isn’t.
VISUALISING AND INTERACTING WITH THE
IMAGINARY WORLD
When you read a novel, you have no autho-
rial control over what you read, but for each
person that visualises what they're reading in
their imagination, there will be a different,
but equally compatible, version of what it
looks like. Your vision of the imaginary
world may include all sorts of details that the
author has neither the space nor inclination
to include (and if they’re a good author,
shouldn’t need to).
When playing an RPG you won't have au-
thorial control either, but that doesn't mean
the way you imagine the world is subject to
the oversight of some authority, like a ‘Game
Master.’ If someone describes something in
the world, then you may interact with it in
the way that you have imagined it, without
having to ask permission, as this is the intu-
itive way that we interact with things in the
real world. If a lake is described in the imagi-
nary world, you do not need to ask if there
are pebbles if it’s reasonable that there would
be some, you can go straight to skimming
them across the water.
This is not an exercise in asking yourself
what you might add to the world, this is just
a case of imagining it in the most intuitive
way, and acting as if it were real. If you do
things this way, the world will feel more real
than if you constantly have to refer to some-
one else's view to know what's there, but just
remember: the goal of this kind of exercise is
verisimilitude - it will be obvious if someone
is trying to bend the fiction to their will in-
stead of adding life and richness to the world.
Life's too short to play with people like that.
So...
If you look through these rules and wonder
why a rule was chosen over [the thing you're
used to] then the answer will probably be: be-
cause it's closer to the human experience of the
thing, or because it's easier to reify, or because
it's more verisimilitudinous.
