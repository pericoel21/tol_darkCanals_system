Let’s face it, 90% of table-top role-playing games are read and not played, and about half of the people reading them are GMs who, like someone reading a novel imagines a fantasy landscape, they sit and imagine what it might be like to play with players that know how to role-play, and the other half are game designers looking to pick the bones for a new mechanic. To cut down on brain activity, people develop labels to categorise these games, so that they can easily dismiss them without actually reading or playing them. Let’s help errant game designers determine what to dismiss Dark Canals as.

GNS THEORY
If at any point while you were reading the
preceding pages you felt cognitive disso-
nance, you may have GNS, or Grognard Syn-
drome. GNS is the belief that there are three
pillars of the gameplay experience that moti-
vate people to play RPGs, and, surprise, sur-
prise, none of them are role-play.
Imagine being told, as a musician, that there
were three reasons to enjoy playing music, 1)
to get paid, 2) to get laid, and 3) to explore
the mathematical relationships of harmo-
nious frequencies. Far be it for us to deny
that musicians deserve to get paid, but it
doesn’t really leave any room for the idea
that playing music might just be inherently
pleasurable, and that we can do it for it’s own
sake. And, that if you don’t enjoy playing mu-
sic, getting paid to do so probably isn’t going
to suddenly make it enjoyable.
Being told as a role-player that you are nec-
essarily a gamist, narrativist or simulationist
(or, if you’re really cool, some combination of
all three) leaves no room for the idea that
role-play can be inherently enjoyable, and
can be done for it’s own sake; the idea that
role-play is just a tool for some other goal,
sells it short.
GAMIST
‘Don’t like your role-playing game to be a game,
eh?’ Don’t be silly, that’s a false dichotomy.
When we complain about things being too
‘gamey’ we’re not decrying games as such,
we’re decrying when game elements hinder
the goals of the game. Like seeing a grand
vista in a video game and feeling the entice-
ment of freedom and exploration only to
butt up against an invisible wall. Game de-
sign is cool, but not when it gets in the way
of the playing, not, in our case, when it gets
in the way of the role-play. Game rules are
important in a sport like football, but the el-
egance of the rules and how they facilitate a
certain kind of play are a scaffold for the ac-
tual playing of the game, they are not the al-
lure of the game itself. Otherwise, why play?
We’d probably get more out of just reading
the rules. Oh, you do? What does that say
about your games, if the scaffolding is the
most stimulating part of them?
NARRATIVIST
Stories are cool too, just not if they hinder
the goal of the game - not if they hinder role-
play! If making decisions is part of role-play-
ing a character, then structures that render
those decisions moot for the sake of a satisfy-
ing narrative are anathema to role-play!
SIMULATIONIST
Realism is cool too, but trying to simulate a
scientific realist view of the universe is going
to fall short in any game. Yet again, you want
to simulate things at the level that facili-
tates role-play, not the level that’s most re-
alistic.
CLASS DISMISSED
No doubt the simulationists would call Dark
Canals narrativist, the narrativists would call
it simulationist, and the gamists, well, they’d
agree with one of the other two guys. Turns
out GNS might just be a false trichotomy,
and that there’s a fourth motivation you can
put at the top.
